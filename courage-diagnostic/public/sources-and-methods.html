01

How to read this page

The calculator's footnote says its figures are "directional industry medians, pending primary-source verification." This page is that verification. Each coefficient is labeled with one of three evidence tiers, and the labels are not generous.

Sourced

The value is taken directly from a named study. You can open the study and find the number.

Bounded

No single study produced the value, but it sits inside a range the published evidence supports, and we show the range.

Judgment call

A modeling choice we made. The evidence explains why the term exists in the model, not why it has this exact value. Argue with these first.

One more label
A coefficient marked Untraced is one we could not connect to any study, even loosely. There is one. It is called out below and in Known weaknesses.
02

The model in one screen

The calculator converts five element scores (0–100) into an annual dollar cost. It is a linear cost model with a nonlinear gap term, a deduplication discount, and a foundation amplifier. Nothing is hidden; this is the whole thing.

// gap: how far each element sits from 100, curved so low scores hurt disproportionately gap(score) = ((100 − score) / 100) ^ 1.5 // Important Work amplifies the other four elements (max ×1.3 when IW = 0) amp = 1 + 0.3 × gap(ImportantWork) // five cost categories (rates apply in full only at a score of 0) FakeWorkTax = payroll × 0.20 × gap(IW) × 0.65 DecisionDrag = payroll × 0.10 × gap(Challenge) × amp × 0.65 PoliticalTax = payroll × 0.12 × gap(Trust) × amp × 0.65 InnovationDeficit = employees × $1,800 × gap(Curiosity) × amp MercenaryCost = employees × 0.15 × 0.63 × salary × 0.85 × gap(Community) × amp // output AnnualCost = sum of the five → shown as mid ± 35% Cumulative = AnnualCost × years → flat, not compounding Recovery = max($150K, 2% × payroll) × (1 + 0.15 × years)

The 0.65 applies only to the three labor-based categories, because they tax the same working hours. Weighted Courage Score = (2×IW + Curiosity + Challenge + Trust + Community) / 6. The optional Candor Adjustment subtracts 10 points from every element before any of this runs.

03

Coefficient ledger

Fourteen numbers drive the calculator. Here is each one, its evidence tier, what the research actually says, and the reference numbers that map to the full citation list.

A. The five cost rates
Fake Work Tax Important Work · labor category
20% of payroll Judgment call

The share of payroll spent on work that exists to look like work: status theater, reporting that reports nothing, initiatives no one believes in. The rate applies in full only at a score of 0 and is then discounted by the 0.65 overlap factor, so the most this category can ever charge is 13% of payroll.

What the evidence says
  • Peterson & Nielson estimate that roughly half of the work employees do fails to advance the organization's strategy [11].
  • Gallup puts the global cost of not-engaged and actively disengaged employees at $8.8 trillion, about 9% of global GDP [12].
  • McKinsey Global Institute estimates that interaction workers could be 20–25% more productive if collaboration and information flow were fixed [13].
Why 20%

It is well under half of the Peterson & Nielson estimate and sits at the bottom of the MGI range. We chose it to be conservative relative to the loudest claims in the literature. It is still our number, not theirs.

References [11] [12] [13]
Innovation Deficit Curiosity · per-employee category
$1,800 / employee Untraced

The annual cost, per person, of ideas that never get raised, questions that never get asked, and information that never crosses a desk because nobody was curious enough to ask for it. Modeled per employee rather than as a share of payroll so it does not scale with salary.

What the evidence says
  • VitalSmarts (n = 1,025) found each avoided crucial conversation costs an average of $7,500 in lost time and resources, and one in five respondents put the cost above $50,000 [8].
  • Panopto (n = 1,000) found employees spend 5.3 hours a week waiting for information from coworkers or recreating information that already exists [14].
  • Zak reports 50% higher self-reported productivity at high-trust companies [3].
Honest status

None of these produce $1,800. The figure is a placeholder chosen to keep Curiosity in the model at a modest weight (about 2.4% of payroll at the default salary, before the gap term). It is the most exposed number in the calculator and the first one we would replace with a defensible derivation. If you have one, we want it.

References [3] [8] [14]
Decision Drag Challenge · labor category
10% of payroll Bounded

The cost of decisions that take too long, get made without the dissent that would have improved them, or get made twice because the first version was consensus theater.

What the evidence says
  • McKinsey (n = 1,259, 91 countries) found respondents spend 37% of their time on decision-making and 61% say most of that time is used ineffectively. For an average Fortune 500 company that works out to roughly 530,000 lost days and $250 million in wasted labor a year, on managers alone [7]. That is about $4,400 per employee per year across the whole headcount.
  • Each avoided crucial conversation costs about $7,500 [8]; 85% of employees in Milliken, Morrison & Hewlin's study had at some point felt unable to raise an issue with a boss [4].
  • Detert & Edmondson show that self-censorship runs on implicit, taken-for-granted rules that operate even when leaders believe speaking up is safe [5].
Why 10%

At a score of 0 and after the overlap discount, this category charges 6.5% of payroll (8.5% with maximum foundation amplification). The McKinsey figure alone, which counts only managers' time, implies roughly 4–6% of a typical payroll. So the ceiling sits modestly above the one hard number we have, and the default score charges well below it.

References [4] [5] [7] [8]
Political Tax Trust · labor category
12% of payroll Bounded

The hours spent on self-protection instead of the work: managing up, managing optics, building the paper trail, and the disengagement that follows when people conclude that leaders don't do what they say.

What the evidence says
  • Zak (n = 1,095 U.S. working adults) reports that people at high-trust companies show 50% higher productivity, 76% more engagement, 74% less stress, 13% fewer sick days and 40% less burnout than people at low-trust companies. All self-reported [3].
  • Simons (6,500 employees, 76 hotels) found that a one-eighth-point improvement in employees' rating of managers' behavioral integrity, meaning whether managers do what they say, was worth about 2.5% of revenue in profit, more than $250,000 per hotel. It was the single strongest predictor of profitability among the manager behaviors measured [1] [2].
  • A meta-analysis in the Academy of Management Journal links perceived organizational politics to lower job satisfaction and commitment, higher strain, and higher turnover intention [6].
  • Watson Wyatt's WorkUSA 2002 reported total shareholder return 286% higher at high-trust organizations. We could only locate this secondhand [15].
Why 12%

Zak's 50% productivity gap and Simons' 2.5%-of-revenue effect both imply that trust is worth more than 12% of payroll at the extremes. We set it below what those studies suggest because both are correlational and one is self-reported. After the overlap discount the ceiling is 7.8% of payroll (10.1% with maximum amplification).

References [1] [2] [3] [6] [15]
Mercenary Cost Community · turnover category
15% × 63% × 85% of salary Mixed

Preventable turnover, priced. Three factors: the share of people who leave in a year, the share of those exits that were preventable, and what it costs to replace each one. Each factor has a different evidence tier.

63% preventable — Sourced

Work Institute's 2025 Retention Report, built from tens of thousands of exit interviews, found that preventable turnover driven by career stagnation, work-life balance and management failures accounted for 63% of all job exits in 2024 [16]. Gallup's 2024 figure is lower: 42% of voluntary leavers said their exit was preventable [17]. We use the higher, exit-interview-based number.

85% of salary to replace — Bounded

Gallup estimates replacement cost at roughly 200% of salary for leaders and managers, 80% for technical roles and 40% for frontline workers [17]. Work Institute's own conservative estimate is 33% of base salary [16]. 85% is a blended mid-workforce figure; an organization that is mostly frontline should lower it, and one that is mostly professional or managerial should raise it.

15% annual turnover — Bounded

BLS JOLTS reports the private-sector quits rate averaged 2.3% per month in 2024, roughly 27% annualized [18]. Employer surveys of voluntary turnover typically land in the low-to-mid teens. 15% is deliberately at the low end; it is the baseline churn the gap term then scales.

Supporting evidence that culture drives exits
  • Toxic culture was 10.4 times more predictive of attrition than compensation during the Great Resignation [19].
  • SHRM estimates culture-driven turnover cost U.S. employers $223 billion over five years; one in five workers left a job because of culture [20].
  • In Korn Ferry's survey of nearly 5,000 professionals, 33% were job-hunting because they were bored and wanted a new challenge, more than cited pay [21].
References [16] [17] [18] [19] [20] [21]
B. Structural parameters
Overlap factor applied to the three labor categories
0.65 Judgment call

Fake Work, Decision Drag and Political Tax all tax the same working hours. An hour spent in a defensive status meeting is simultaneously fake work, decision drag and political cover. Summing the three raw rates (42%) would count that hour three times, so the sum is discounted to 27.3%. No study produced 0.65; it is a deduplication assumption. Because the labor categories are about three-quarters of the total at the default inputs, changing this factor moves the total almost proportionally.

Gap exponent gap = ((100 − score)/100)^1.5
1.5 Judgment call

Trust does not erode in a straight line; it holds, then breaks. The exponent makes low scores hurt disproportionately and high scores cost almost nothing. A score of 52 is charged 33% of the ceiling rather than the 48% a linear model would charge; a score of 80 is charged 9% rather than 20%. The direction is supported by Slovic's asymmetry principle (trust is built slowly and destroyed quickly) [10] and by Morrison & Milliken's account of how climates of silence become self-reinforcing [9]. The value 1.5 is ours.

References [9] [10]
Foundation weighting Important Work: 2× in the score, amp = 1 + 0.3 × gap(IW)
2× / up to 1.3× Judgment call

This encodes the framework's central claim: when work loses its weight, dishonesty about it carries no cost, so every other gap gets more expensive. Important Work counts double in the weighted score, and its gap amplifies the other four cost categories by up to 30%. Korn Ferry's finding that boredom and lack of challenge outrank pay as reasons to leave [21] points the same direction, but the 2× and 0.3 are framework-driven choices, not measured effects.

Reference [21]; framework: The Courage Economy
Output range shown around every total
± 35% Judgment call

The range exists so the calculator never publishes a false-precision point estimate. It is not a computed confidence interval; it is a stated uncertainty band that we think is, if anything, too narrow given the tiers above.

Recovery cost base and annual escalation
max($150K, 2% of payroll) · +15%/yr Judgment call

The base is a rough floor for a serious leadership-team and culture intervention at a mid-sized organization. The escalation encodes a pattern the attrition research supports in direction, that truth-tellers leave first and new hires get socialized into the theater [19], but no study measures it at 15% a year. Treat this as an illustration of the argument that delay is not free, not as a forecast.

Reference [19]
Cumulative cost annual cost × years
flat, not compounding Judgment call

Simple accumulation, deliberately. We do not compound the annual drag, because we have no evidence that it grows year over year at a knowable rate. This is the conservative choice and it makes the "left on the table" figure a floor.

Candor Adjustment optional self-report correction
−10 points Judgment call

Organizations deep in a Lie Economy inflate their own scores; that is what a Lie Economy is. Sull, Turconi & Sull found no correlation between the values companies publish and the culture their employees describe [22], and Detert & Edmondson show that people self-censor under rules leaders do not know exist [5]. Both justify the existence of the toggle. The size of the correction, 10 points, is ours.

References [5] [22]
Zone labels & defaults 20 / 40 / 60 / 80 · 250 employees · $75,000 · 3 years
descriptive Judgment call

The zone thresholds (Lie Economy, Fear-Based, Guarded, Cautious Courage, Courage Economy) are labels at even intervals, not empirically derived cut points. The default inputs are illustrative and every one of them is editable.

What the model actually charges

The headline rates (20%, 10%, 12%) are ceilings that apply only at a score of zero. The gap curve, the overlap discount and the amplifier all pull the effective charge down from there. How that compares to the evidence
At the default score of 52, the model charges about 13% of payroll, or roughly $10,000 per employee. For reference: Gallup's disengagement estimate is 9% of global GDP [12]; McKinsey's decision-time figure alone is about $4,400 per employee per year, counting only managers' hours [7]; one avoided crucial conversation costs $7,500 [8]; and Simons' integrity effect is worth about 2.5% of revenue, which for a labor-heavy business is a large share of payroll [1]. The default charge is inside the envelope those numbers draw. The ceiling is not.
On the ceiling
The calculator's own code comments describe a "credible ceiling" of about 27% of payroll. That figure is the three labor categories after the overlap discount and before the foundation amplifier. With the amplifier at its maximum the labor ceiling is 31.6%, and the full model, including turnover and innovation, reaches about 45% of payroll at a score of zero across the board. A score of zero on all five elements is not a real organization; it is the mathematical edge of the model. But the 27% figure should be read as what it is: the labor-only, pre-amplifier ceiling.

06

Known weaknesses

The Skeptic's Appendix answers the six strongest objections to the calculator's existence. This list is narrower: the specific places where the numbers are weaker than the page makes them look.

1
The $1,800 Innovation Deficit is untraced. No study we found produces it. It should be replaced with a derivation, for example a per-employee share of the VitalSmarts avoided-conversation cost, or dropped and folded into Decision Drag.
2
Most coefficients are ours. Of fourteen, one factor (63% preventable) is taken directly from a study. Five are bounded by evidence. The rest are modeling choices. That is a defensible way to build a directional tool; it is not the same as "grounded in research," and the calculator's footnote should say which is which.
3
The evidence is correlational and much of it is self-reported. Zak's productivity gap is what employees said about themselves. Simons is the strongest causal-leaning study here and it is one industry. None of the sources establish that raising a Courage Score causes the modeled savings.
4
Replacement cost is set well above Work Institute's own figure. We cite Work Institute for the 63% and then price replacement at 85% of salary, when their report says 33%. The 85% comes from Gallup's role-based range. Both are legitimate; using them together should be visible, and now it is.
5
The "27% ceiling" is understated. The code comments and the Skeptic's Appendix cite a credible ceiling of about 27% of payroll. That is the labor categories before the foundation amplifier. The model's actual ceiling is 31.6% for labor and roughly 45% in total. See What it actually charges.
6
Two attributions in the calculator footnote are imprecise. The decision-making research is from McKinsey's Organization Practice, not the McKinsey Global Institute. The organizational-silence paper is in the Academy of Management Review; the Journal articles are Detert & Edmondson and Chang, Rosen & Levy.
7
Some sources are secondhand or vendor-produced. Watson Wyatt (2002) and Panopto (2018) could only be reached through secondary reporting; VitalSmarts and Work Institute are firms that sell solutions to the problems they measure. They are cited because the numbers are widely used, not because they are peer-reviewed.
8
Recovery cost and its 15% escalation are illustrative. No study measures how fast the cost of reversing a Lie Economy grows. The figure exists to make the argument that delay is not free. It should not be quoted as a forecast.

Found a better number?

Every figure on this page is open to challenge. Send the study and we will update the model and this page.
